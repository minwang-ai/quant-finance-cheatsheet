    Summary¶

    \(f,g: \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{+}\) map natural numbers to positive real numbers.

    Notation Asymptotically (approximately) Formal Definition Intuition Limit Test
    (as \(n \to \infty\))    		 Important Example
    \(f \sim g\)
    tilde    		 \(f = g\) same as the limit test Equal
    - cannot ignore constant factor    		 \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = \color{blue}1\) - Apply integral method to approximate Harmonic Numbers \(H_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{i} \sim \ln n\)

    - Approximating Products (e.g., n factorial)
    Stirling’s approximation: \(n! \sim \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n \sqrt{2\pi n}\) More precise bound: \(e^{\frac{1}{12n+1}} \le \frac{n!}{\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n \sqrt{2\pi n}} \le e^{\frac{1}{12n}}\)
    \(f \in O(g)\)
    Big-O    		 \(f \le g\) \(\exists \text{ constant }c > 0, \text{ starting point } n_0 \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. } \forall n \ge n_0, \; \|f(n)\| \le c \cdot g(n)\) \(O(g)\) is a set of functions that are asymptotically upper bounded by \(g\) (showing worst case)
    - Ignore lower-order terms and constant factors (they are absorbed into constant \(c\))    		 \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} \color{blue} < \infty\)
    Note:
    -if the limit \(=\infty\) then \(f \notin O(g)\)
    - if the limit does not exist, inconclusive then we must go back to the definition and find \(n_0\) and \(c\)    		 \(n^2 + 3n + 7 \in O(n^2)\)
    \(1000 \in O(n^2)\)
    \(f \in \Omega(g)\)
    Big-Omega    		 \(f \ge g\) \(g \in O(f)\) \(\Omega(g)\) is the set of functions that are asymptotically lower bounded by g (showing best case)
    - Ignore lower-order terms and constant factors    		 \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} \color{blue}\in (0, \infty ]\) \(x^3 \in \Omega(x^2)\)
    \(5x^2 \in \Omega(x^2)\)
    \(f \in \Theta(g)\)
    Big-Theta    		 \(f = g\) \(f \in O(g) \text{ and } f \in \Omega(g)\) \(\Theta(g)\) is the set of functions that are asymptotically equal to \(g\) (showing average case; both upper bound and lower bound) \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} \color{blue}\in (0, \infty)\) \(3 + sin(n) \in \Theta(1)\)
    \(n! \sim \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n \sqrt{2\pi n}\\ \implies n! = \Theta\left(\sqrt n (\frac{n}{e})^n\right)\\ \implies \log(n!)= \Theta(n \log n)\)
    \(f \in o(g)\)
    Little-o    		 \(f < g\)
    much smaller    		 same as limit test \(o(g)\) is the set of functions that are asymptotically much smaller to \(g\)
    - Little o can explain the lower order term \(h\) which can be ignored \(f \sim f +h \iff h\in o(f) \iff \lim_{n\to \infty} \frac{h}{f}=0\)    		 \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = \color{blue}0\) \((\log n)^a = o(n^b), \space \forall \text{constant } a, b > 0\)
    \(f \in \omega(g)\)
    Little-omega    		 \(f > g\)
    much bigger    		 \(g \in o(f)\) \(\omega(g)\) is the set of functions that are asymptotically much bigger to \(g\) \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = \color{blue}\infty\) \(x^2 \in \omega(x)\)
    \(2^x \in \omega(x^{100})\)

    Figure: Big O notation - \(O(g)\) is a set of functions that are asymptotically upper bounded by \(g\), \(f \in O(g)\) means \(f\) one of the functions in the set \(O(g)\)

    Figure: Big Omega notation - \(\Omega(g)\) is a set of functions that are asymptotically lower bounded by \(g\), \(f \in \Omega(g)\) means \(f\) is one of the functions in the set \(\Omega(g)\)

    Figure: Big Theta notation - \(\Theta(g)\) is a set of functions that are asymptotically equal to \(g\), \(f \in \Theta(g)\) means \(f\) is one of the functions in the set \(\Theta(g)\)

    The relationships of asymptotics¶

    according to limit test e.g., Little \(o\) implies big \(O\)

    \[ \begin{array}{ccccccc} \sim & \Longrightarrow & \Theta & \Longrightarrow & O & \text{and} & \Omega \\ & & & & \Uparrow & & \Uparrow \\ & & & & o & & \omega \end{array} \]

    Warning: Do not mix asymptotic notation and induction.¶

    Example: False Theorem: \(2^n \in O(1)\)

    False proof. Proof by induction.

    • Base case: when \(n=0\), \(2^0=1 \in O(1)\)
    • Inductive step: assume \(n \ge 0\), \(2^n \in O(1)\), want to show \(2^{n+1}\in O(1)\): \(2^{n+1}=2^n + 2^n \in O(1) + O(1) = O(1)\) Done.

    The reason why induction fails: We prove something completely different: \(2^0\in O(1), 2^1\in O(1), 2^2\in O(1), 2^3\in O(1), \ldots\) but we cannot treat a function \(2^n\) as \(n\) individual constant functions.
